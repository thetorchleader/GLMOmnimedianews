Fifty Years After Lausanne, Gen Z Says the Covenant Still Speaks: Word and Deed Together

By GLM Omnimedia News November 12, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

Fifty years ago, delegates from 150 nations gathered in Lausanne, Switzerland, and wrote a document that would shape world evangelization for generations. The Lausanne Covenant, a modern evangelical creed focused on carrying the Gospel to every nation, has since been adopted by hundreds of organizations and thousands of churches to define their beliefs, their partnerships, and their very identity.

Billy Graham, who helped found the Lausanne Movement at that 1974 congress, later reflected that his involvement with Lausanne was possibly the most significant contribution of his career. That is a large claim. Now a new generation is asking how the covenant measures up today.

According to Christianity Today, six young representatives — all members of Generation Z and millennials, each from a different continent — recently gathered at the invitation of the Lausanne Movement to spend four days discussing the legacy of the founding document and what it means for the global church's next generation. The author, Rome Williams, a 24-year-old Master of Divinity student at Beeson Divinity School, was one of them.

Their first conclusion was that the covenant assumes a divide between evangelism and social action that no longer holds. Though the covenant expresses penitence for having “sometimes regarded evangelism and social concern as mutually exclusive,” the young leaders said it still wrongly separates the two. As they see it, there is no tension between word and deed: to declare the good news of God's atonement for human sin through Christ's sacrifice without displaying the kingdom work of justice and reconciliation — or the reverse — is to express an incomplete Gospel.

At first, the group found the covenant's language on this point stale, with its careful qualifications that “reconciliation with other people is not reconciliation with God, nor is social action evangelism, nor is political liberation salvation.” Then it dawned on them: the covenant has influenced global Christian mission for 50 years, and to judge it as overly simple in light of current mission practice is to critique it for being successful. If its ideas on social action now feel obvious, that is precisely because they have become standard.

The group did find one clear flaw: the covenant downplays discipleship, prioritizing evangelism over it. They agreed that evangelism is always a means to the end of discipleship. In their summary, the covenant fails to unite evangelism, social action, and discipleship as a clear trinity of Christian witness — each distinct, necessary, and inseparable from the others. To sacrifice one is to cripple them all.

But the covenant's call to repentance, they said, still stands in full force: “We confess with shame that we have often denied our calling and failed in our mission, by becoming conformed to the world or by withdrawing from it.” Lament and repentance are foundational to the church's witness, Williams wrote, and his generation needs the church to return to that foundation after so many leadership scandals and so much polarization.

The covenant's warning against political compromise also reads as almost prophetic to the young leaders: “The church is the community of God's people rather than an institution, and must not be identified with any particular culture, social or political system, or human ideology.” Originally penned against colonialism and communism, the statement still condemns the church's politicization today. As Williams put it, his generation is tired of churches that are Trojan horses for donkeys and elephants.

Finally, the covenant insists that community is not optional: “Jesus still calls all who would follow him to … identify themselves with his new community.” The goal of evangelism is not only to bring non-Christians to a new faith, but to introduce them to a new community. Becoming a Christian is a call to belong as much as to believe.

The Lausanne Covenant was never intended to be merely a statement of belief, but a commitment to action — to affirm it is to commit to live by its principles. Though the six representatives came from different countries, spoke different languages, and worshiped in different denominations, Williams wrote that the committed Christianity of the covenant offered them all a better way of following Jesus. At the end of their time together, they made the commitment it implicitly demands — and invited the rest of the church to make the same commitment, pursuing repentance, rejecting compromise, and serving Christ in word and deed together.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

Jesus commanded, &ldquo;Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you&rdquo; (Matthew 28:19-20). The Great Commission was never only about preaching — it is about making disciples, and disciples are made in word and in deed, together, in the community of the church.
