Being Normal at Thanksgiving: Students on Faith, Politics, and Loving Family Around the Table

By GLM Omnimedia News November 25, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

Thanksgiving brings the family together around one table — and with the family come the topics many would rather leave at the door. In Christianity Today's student column, The Syllabus, evangelical students were asked what topic they regularly talk about with friends but would not bring up at their family's house during Thanksgiving, and how they change the subject when the conversation gets tense. Their answers are honest, gentle, and worth hearing the day before the holiday.

For Isaiah Bulman, a youth pastor and graduate student at Baylor University's Truett Seminary, religion is the topic he cannot avoid — his work is the ministry, so if anyone asks about school or work, the conversation turns to God. He told the column that his family members have had two reactions: they either avoid God-talk altogether, or they will not stop talking about it. His way through is simple and kind: if someone seems nervous to talk about his job, he tells a funny story; if someone goes on a theology rant, he smiles and asks them about themselves. “People love to talk about themselves,” he said. His plea is a plain one: if you have pastors in the family this Thanksgiving, treat them like humans. Talk about sports, your work, or simply goof off. Ministers are humans too, and they want to be treated like one.

Ryan Hendrick, a senior at Baylor University, said his family steers clear of politics. His family loves and respects each other, he said, but because family members hold very different political views, they tend to avoid those conversations at Thanksgiving. With his college friends, talking politics is laid back and open-minded — if someone disagrees, it is no big deal. With family, though, sharing a view can feel like picking a side against another family member. Since he only gets to visit home a couple of times a year, he would rather keep the conversation peaceful and enjoy the time they have together.

Kendall Petteway, also a graduate student at Truett Seminary and an aspiring pastor, said the theological debates belong in the seminary classroom, not at the Thanksgiving table. He spends his week talking about what he believes, how he interprets Scripture, and how he understands God, and those conversations often invite healthy disagreement. But he has learned, through experience, that not every conversation that belongs in a classroom belongs at the table. He put the question this way: at what point does our desire to be right become greater than our desire to love? Christians should be willing to engage in difficult conversations, he said, but there is a difference between knowing how to respond and having the wisdom to discern whether we should. Scripture reminds us to be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to anger (James 1:19). When a conversation grows tense, he redirects it toward something the family can appreciate together — a shared memory, a moment of gratitude, or simply asking someone about their life. Thanksgiving, he said, should not become a chance to show how much theology we know while forgetting how deeply we are called to love one another.

Owen Witschonke, a junior at Baylor University, sees the beauty of the differences themselves. His family gathers around the television every year to watch A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving, and though the feast in that special was only jellybeans and popcorn, it showed something real: people coming together, despite their differences, to share a meal and express gratitude. His family is spread across the country, and the differences in where they live bring different perspectives and ideas to the table, which often leads to engaging conversations. While they may not always see eye to eye, he said he has never seen a Thanksgiving gathering turn hostile. The book of Romans speaks to this very idea, he said: we are not called to be exactly like one another, but we are called to build up the body of Christ. By choosing to focus on the positive aspects of their differences, he gets to see the beauty of being uniquely created.

These four students, in their own words, point to the same truth. The Thanksgiving table is not a courtroom and not a classroom. It is a table where a family that does not agree on everything can still sit down together, bow their heads, give thanks, and enjoy one another. Being right is a small thing next to being loving. And when the conversation turns tense — as it sometimes will — the faithful move is not another argument but a question, a memory, a word of thanks. Gratitude has a way of making room at the table for everyone.

As families gather this Thanksgiving, the counsel of these young believers is worth carrying to the table: be swift to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger; let your speech be seasoned with grace; and let love, not the last word, have the final say.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

The Thanksgiving table is a place for grace before it is a place for debate. “Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath” (James 1:19). Listen first, love always, and let gratitude set the tone.
