Jordanian Christians Grieve Karak Church Attack and Answer With Scripture, Solidarity, and Hope

By GLM Omnimedia News November 16, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

KARAK, Jordan — Two weeks after gunmen opened fire during a funeral at a red-domed Greek Orthodox church in the village of Hamoud, Jordanian Christians are still grieving — and still standing. Nine people died and at least 34 were wounded in the attack, which Christianity Today reports was the worst terrorist attack in Jordan in 10 years.

The service had gathered about 150 mourners to bury a beloved local woman when three gunmen entered the church courtyard and opened fire, according to the report. A man identified only as Faris, whose name is a pseudonym, was outside with other men when the shooting began. He dropped to his knees and crawled to safety before scaling a wall as bodies fell around him.

Munther Namat, general secretary of the Bible Society of Jordan and a relative of Faris, told Christianity Today he first assumed the shooting must have been a tribal dispute. A call to a priest friend in Karak confirmed it was terrorism. "We watched the videos. We saw the blood," he said. "Two weeks later, these images still keep me awake at night. A church should be the safest place on earth."

Namat said he was himself attending a funeral that day — his 24-year-old cousin had died — when the news arrived. Within half an hour, he said, police reached the location where he was, as they did at other services that day. Roads were shut down as security forces moved to apprehend those responsible. Police later arrested three suspects, the army struck an alleged terrorist camp across the border in Syria, and the government proclaimed three days of mourning, with Jordanian flags draped over the victims' coffins at a large funeral service.

King Abdullah II, dressed in military fatigues, visited a military hospital where some of the wounded were being treated and vowed to use "an iron fist" against terrorism. Jordan's interior minister, Mazin Al Farrayeh, also rejected the use of old legal language that described Christians as a protected second-class community, and instead emphasized the concept of citizenship.

That word — citizenship — is one Namat and other Jordanian Christians are now pressing. "We want to be 'Jordanian' regardless of religion," he said. "We are an integral part of our national history, identity, and future." Some Christians, he said, are starting to reject the word "coexistence" in favor of "citizenship," because coexistence can sound like two separate peoples tolerating each other, while citizenship says they belong to one nation together.

The response from Jordanian society has also included quiet acts of solidarity. One church leader said Muslims were the first to donate blood after the rampage. Namat was careful to draw a line between the attackers and his neighbors. "Our grief and anger should not be mistaken as a rejection of our fellow citizens," he said. "Jordan is a model for relations between Muslims and Christians. We distinguish between the ideology of the attackers and the values of Jordanian society, which has shown genuine solidarity with us."

Christians number around 200,000 out of a population of 10 million in Jordan, and the country ranks No. 49 on Open Doors' list of places where it is hardest to be a believer. The attack in Karak, a region home to a sizable Christian community, was the second major attack there in a decade; a 2016 attack killed 14 tourists and police officers.

By the Sunday after the attack, the Bible Society was already at work. Namat said his team prepared a bouquet of flowers in the shape of a cross and banners of solidarity for the families, and by Monday had produced a 30-page booklet of Bible verses addressing the hardest questions the attack raises: "Lord, do you see my tears?" "If you are good, why did you allow this evil?" "Does their story end at the grave?" The cover bears the faces of the nine victims. Three thousand copies were printed for distribution in Karak, and 10,000 more have since gone out to memorial services in Amman, through churches, and online. The society is also offering trauma healing services for survivors and affected families.

Asked what he wants Western Christians to understand, Namat said Jordan is not immune to the pressures of the region, but its Christians are committed to their country's future. He asked for support not only for Jordanian Christians but for King Abdullah, "to strengthen our nation," and pointed to the king's words at the United Nations: "We are protecting Christianity — its communities and heritage — in its native and historic land." "After God," Namat said, "we are in good hands with our king."

The Bible Society says it remains committed to sharing God's Word and the message of Jesus Christ, trusting in the power of the gospel to bring repentance, healing, and hope where violence has caused so much pain.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

A church should be the safest place on earth, and when violence shatters that safety, the answer of the faithful is not hatred but grief carried to God, care for the wounded, and a steady witness to the hope of the gospel. "Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted" (Matthew 5:4). The God of all comfort comforts us in our affliction so that we may comfort others (2 Corinthians 1:3-4).
