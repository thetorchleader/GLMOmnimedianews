Maine Mother Asks Supreme Court to Review Order Barring Her From Taking Daughter to Church

By GLM Omnimedia News November 27, 2026 Source: Christian Post

A Maine mother is asking the United States Supreme Court to step in after the state's highest court upheld a custody order that gives her daughter's father the final say over whether their 13-year-old attends her mother's church — and, according to her lawyers, bars the girl from being exposed to the Bible without the father's approval.

Liberty Counsel filed the petition last week on behalf of Emily Bickford, asking the justices to review the Maine Supreme Judicial Court's September ruling in Bickford v. Bradeen. The Supreme Court has not yet announced whether it will take the case.

Mat Staver, Liberty Counsel's founder and chairman, said the Maine ruling "bars a fit parent from teaching her daughter her Christian faith or exposing her to any religious philosophy or the Bible in general," and warned that it "abandons the constitutional safeguards that protect parental rights and religious liberty." He added that if the ruling stands, it creates a precedent under which courts could suppress religious expression in a parent-child relationship without the highest level of scrutiny the Constitution ordinarily requires.

The petition asks the justices to decide whether a court may subordinate a fit parent's religious freedom to a subjective judgment about a child's best interests when parents disagree about religious upbringing. It also asks whether such a custody order violates the First Amendment's Free Exercise Clause when it prevents a fit parent from exercising her Christian faith with her child and, in the petition's words, shows "disagreement at best, or hostility at worst, about Christian doctrine and viewpoints."

The dispute began in 2021, when Bickford started attending services at Calvary Chapel Greater Portland with her daughter. The girl's father, Bradeen, who never married Bickford, learned of the child's participation in a church event and raised concerns about the church's teachings, including the subjects of Hell and the Second Coming of Christ. He said the child had suffered panic attacks over those teachings and had worried that her father would be left behind when the Second Coming occurs. The parents also disagreed over their daughter's medical care, including vaccinations and the use of antibiotics.

In its unanimous September 1 ruling, the Maine Supreme Judicial Court affirmed the lower court's decision. Chief Justice Valerie Stanfill wrote that parents have a fundamental constitutional right to direct their children's upbringing, including in religious matters, but emphasized that both parents share that right. "An allocation of rights to one parent inherently limits the authority of the other but does not necessarily infringe upon the constitutional rights of either parent," she wrote. The court said parental rights and responsibilities "must be allocated in whatever fashion best serves the best interest of the child."

Bickford's lawyers had argued that the lower court showed hostility toward her religion, pointing to its use of the word "cult" in describing her church. The state high court was unpersuaded, noting that the lower court had used the term when summarizing the testimony of Janja Lalich, a sociologist who testified as an expert witness for Bradeen, rather than labeling the church a cult itself. The court also said the lower court's concerns centered on messages that portrayed Bradeen as an enemy who threatened his daughter's faith, and that the ruling did not declare the Christian religion psychologically harmful. Bickford's attorneys dispute that reading, saying the district court "denigrated Bickford's religious beliefs as 'cultic' and psychologically harmful, and then used that characterization to strip Bickford of her fundamental right to direct the religious upbringing of her daughter."

Michelle King, an attorney for Bradeen, called the state high court's decision "well thought-out and reasoned" when it was issued in September and said she had anticipated all along that the case could reach the U.S. Supreme Court.

The petition argues that "until the decision below, no state court of last resort had ever sustained a total prohibition on a fit parent's religious exercise with her child under the best interest standard," and that "Maine stands isolated." As the filing puts it, "a parent's ability to take her child to church during her own parenting time now depends on the State in which she lives." Whether the Supreme Court agrees to hear the case — and, if it does, how it rules — will matter far beyond one Maine family, because the answer will shape how much freedom every parent has to share the faith with their own child.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

God gave parents — not the state — the first charge over their children's souls: "And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way" (Deuteronomy 6:7). Whatever the Court decides, Christians should pray for this mother and daughter, for the father, and for the justices, that the freedom to bring a child to church and open the Bible with her would be protected for every family (Proverbs 22:6).
