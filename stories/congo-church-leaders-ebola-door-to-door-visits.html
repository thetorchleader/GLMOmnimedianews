In Congo's Ebola Outbreak, Church Leaders Go Door to Door to Save Lives

By GLM Omnimedia News November 22, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

In the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, where fear and mistrust have kept some families from seeking help during a deadly Ebola outbreak, church leaders are walking from house to house, pleading with their neighbors to trust the health workers and let the sick be tested and treated.

Patrick Lokombe, an Anglican lay minister in the city of Beni in North Kivu Province, said some communities have resisted medical teams, in part over restrictions on traditional burial practices. Religious leaders, he said, have been able to enter villages where health workers face hostility.

"We have been able to go inside villages, talk to people about the risks of Ebola and they finally agree to give us those suspected to have the virus so that they can go and get tested," Lokombe said. "This has saved a lot of lives."

The outbreak, caused by the Bundibugyo virus, was declared in May and has now touched eight of Congo's 26 provinces. As of November 9, the country had recorded 11,042 confirmed cases and 5,387 deaths, according to the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, with cases still rising at a record pace. It is now the second-largest Ebola outbreak on record, after the 2014-16 epidemic in West Africa.

The disease has also crossed borders. Uganda recorded 20 confirmed cases and two deaths and has since brought its outbreak under control, while Kenya reported nine cases and two deaths and discharged its last patient in November, beginning a 42-day monitoring period before it can be declared Ebola-free.

Francis Mbombo, an evangelical preacher based in Goma, the capital of North Kivu Province near the Rwandan border, said pastors and other faith leaders have organized awareness campaigns, countered misinformation, identified suspected infections, and urged residents to seek treatment rather than remain at home. Training from organizations such as the African Christian Health Associations Platform has equipped some religious leaders to recognize possible Ebola symptoms, protect themselves, and report suspected infections to medical authorities.

"We have tracked hundreds of contacts and taken them to health facilities to avoid the spread of the disease, and this has saved lives," Mbombo said. Archbishop Martin Gordon of the Anglican Diocese of Goma put it simply: "The communities trust church leaders to tell them the truth."

One of the hardest parts of the work, Lokombe said, has been persuading families to accept limits on traditional funeral practices. In some communities, relatives gather around a loved one who has died, touch the body, or wash it before burial. Those practices carry grave risk when a person has died of Ebola, because the virus spreads through contact with infected bodily fluids. The restrictions have also caused resentment, with some families feeling denied the chance to mourn according to their customs. Lokombe said religious leaders have spent months explaining the risks and encouraging families to accept safer burials without abandoning the duty to mourn and honor the dead.

The World Health Organization, in its November 6 update, reported that some affected areas had seen declines in cases, though transmission remained high nationally. The improvements cannot be credited to religious leaders alone, and there are no independently verified figures showing how many deaths their work has prevented. But the leaders interviewed said their efforts have helped residents seek medical care and cooperate with health teams.

The health emergency is unfolding in the middle of decades of violence in eastern Congo, where armed groups have fought over territory, political influence, and valuable mineral resources, forcing millions of people from their homes. The M23 rebel movement, which seized Goma and other strategic areas in 2025, has been accused by the United States and United Nations experts of receiving military support from neighboring Rwanda. Kigali has denied backing the rebels. The Trump administration brokered a peace agreement between Congo and Rwanda in December 2025, but fighting continued, and in October Washington imposed sanctions on Rwanda's Ministry of Defence, accusing it of supporting M23 with military equipment, training, and logistical assistance.

The Rev. Olivier Ndondo, provincial superior of the Congregation of the Blessed Sacrament in Congo and Uganda, said in an interview published October 13 by the Catholic charity Aid to the Church in Need that forced displacement is making the outbreak harder to contain, as families fleeing the fighting move between communities, including across the border with Uganda. He said the Catholic Church is responding by welcoming displaced people, providing emergency assistance, and raising awareness about the disease despite limited resources. "The Church in the east is facing both emergencies, the war and the epidemic with the resources it has and whatever support it can mobilize," he said.

For the religious leaders on the ground, the outbreak has added heavy new duties at a time when insecurity has already strained their churches and the families they serve. Mbombo said faith leaders need continued training and stronger cooperation with health authorities, especially in villages that remain difficult for medical teams to reach. Their efforts, however, cannot replace functioning hospitals, trained health workers, or adequate medical supplies, and the high number of deaths occurring outside treatment centers remains a serious concern. "With war, we lose hope," Ndondo said.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

When fear keeps the suffering from help, the church walks through the door anyway. "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2). These pastors in Congo are carrying their neighbors' burdens in the most literal way — and the body of Christ around the world can carry them in prayer, that God would stop the plague, end the war, and strengthen the hands of those who serve.
