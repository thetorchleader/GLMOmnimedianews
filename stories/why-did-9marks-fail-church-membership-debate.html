Has 9Marks Failed? New Essay Says Church Health Needs More Than Good Theology

By GLM Omnimedia News November 5, 2026 Source: Protestia

A new essay is asking a hard question for one of the most influential church-health movements of the last generation: after more than a quarter century of work, has 9Marks actually made churches healthier?

Pastor and writer John B. Carpenter, writing for Protestia, says he was an early and eager supporter of the movement. He bought its books, gave them away, and taught the material. But he was surprised when religious statistician Ryan Burge reported that average weekly attendance as a share of reported Southern Baptist Convention membership had, by 2023, returned to roughly where it stood before 9Marks began — about 31 percent. More recent figures put the ratio at approximately 36 percent in 2025, still about the same as when 9Marks was founded in 1998.

Over that same period, Carpenter notes, Southern Baptist membership has declined by more than 20 percent. Some of that decline reflects the deaths of faithful older members, he acknowledges, and some reflects churches removing inactive members from their rolls or former members leaving the faith altogether. The statistics alone cannot prove what 9Marks caused or prevented, and Carpenter is careful to say so. But he argues the broader recovery of meaningful membership the movement sought has not materialized on a denomination-wide scale.

Carpenter is fair to the other side: defenders of 9Marks could argue that without the movement, the attendance-to-membership ratio would be even lower — that 9Marks slowed a decline it could not stop. He concedes that counterfactual cannot be measured. His question is different: if 9Marks exists to promote healthy churches, and the chief measure of church health shows no improvement, what are we to think?

His answer centers on what he calls the gap between theology and practice. The nine marks themselves are largely specific practices — expository preaching, church discipline, meaningful membership — and Carpenter praises the movement’s books as theologically strong. But he argues much of its teaching has remained abstract, addressing the ideas behind behavior while leaving the concrete habits of congregational life underemphasized. Christians, he writes, learn through the practices that embody doctrine: they learn about conversion through baptism, about Christ’s sacrifice through the Lord’s Supper, and about walking together in Christian love through church covenants — actual documents believers subscribe to, recite, and are expected to follow.

Carpenter points in particular to the church covenant, which he says was understood for the first 300 years of congregational life as a constitutive act of the local church. Searching 9Marks’ “Pastors Talk” podcast for discussion of church covenants, he found no dedicated episode through nearly 250 programs — an absence he calls telling. He also criticizes 9Marks author Jonathan Leeman’s counsel that churches should “bless” members who leave even for what Leeman calls “foolish reasons,” arguing that such advice undercuts the very covenants the movement praises.

The essay also charges 9Marks with what Carpenter calls the “tyranny of the agreeable” — a reluctance to be controversial that, in his view, keeps its reputation high among evangelical leaders while leaving hard problems unsolved. He recounts how 9Marks founder Mark Dever forcefully defended congregationalism at a 2022 conference, was criticized for his tone, and later apologized publicly. Carpenter admires the humility of the apology but argues Dever was not at fault, and that the episode risks teaching reform-minded pastors that forcefulness in defense of necessary reform is a greater concern than the deficiencies being confronted.

Carpenter closes with hope rather than dismissal. It is not too late, he writes, to reverse course: teach congregations to covenant together, hold members accountable to their promises, sing more psalms, and restore responsible transfers between churches through letters of dismission. Defend those practices when they become unpopular. “Meaningful membership,” he concludes, “requires promises that mean something.”

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

Good doctrine must be lived, not only believed. Scripture calls believers to be “doers of the word, and not hearers only” (James 1:22) — and a healthy church is built when its members keep the promises they make to one another before God.
