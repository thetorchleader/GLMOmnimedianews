Hunger Doesn't Take a Holiday. Neither Should the Church.

By GLM Omnimedia News November 26, 2026 Source: CBN News

As families across America gather around full tables this Thanksgiving, millions of their neighbors are wondering how they will put food on the table at all. Grocery prices remain high, lines at food banks and local pantries are growing longer, and churches are hearing more requests for help from desperate families. Hunger, as CBN News contributor Wendell Vinson writes, does not take a holiday — and neither should the church's responsibility to care for people who are experiencing it.

Vinson, co-founder and president of CityServe International, says he has spoken with pastors and nonprofit leaders from coast to coast who all say the same thing: the hunger crisis never went away. He notes that hunger affects almost eight percent of the globe. It is not a distant problem or an abstract debate. It is a mother deciding which bill must go unpaid so her children do not skip lunch. It is a grandparent raising grandchildren on a fixed income that no longer stretches far enough. It is a household one car repair away from an empty pantry.

These are not strangers. They are neighbors, co-workers, and the family sitting a pew away on Sunday morning — possibly struggling in silence. That is why the need cannot be met by any one organization alone. Churches, communities, and neighbors must step in together, one plate, one bag of groceries, one relationship at a time.

Vinson argues that the local church is uniquely positioned to step into the gap — not to replace the food banks and pantries already doing this work, but to stand with them. The Scripture he points to is direct. Jesus said, "For I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink" (Matthew 25:35). The Lord did not hand the hungry over to a legislator or a system; He handed them to His people. James puts it just as plainly: "If a brother or sister be naked, and destitute of daily food, and one of you say unto them, Depart in peace, be ye warmed and filled; notwithstanding ye give them not those things which are needful to the body; what doth it profit?" (James 2:15-16).

That conviction is at the heart of CityServe's compassion initiative for the hungry, one of ten initiatives the organization runs to help churches around the world turn good intentions into hands-on solutions. The strategy is to equip local congregations to feed people who are food insecure up close, where human connection goes further than a handout.

A church does not have to start from zero. Most communities already have a food bank doing remarkable work with limited resources. The simplest step, Vinson writes, is to call and ask what is needed. Sometimes it is volunteers to sort and pack. Sometimes it is a truck and a few strong backs on distribution day. Sometimes it is opening the church parking lot as a drop site, so distance is not an obstacle.

What the church adds is not just more food. It is a name remembered, a conversation, a prayer, and an open door someone is invited to walk back through. The impact reaches beyond meals served: a child able to focus on learning instead of an empty stomach, or a parent finding breathing room in a difficult season.

Timing matters. As the holiday season begins, food pantries often face some of their greatest deficits of the year. The simple acts Vinson suggests are within anyone's reach: add a few extra groceries to the cart, offer time at a food distribution event, or invite a neighbor in need to the table.

The task is large and growing, but so is the church's capacity to rise to it — if God's people are willing to step in, not to wait for someone else to solve hunger, but to be part of the answer right where they are.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

Feeding the hungry is not someone else's job. "For I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat" (Matthew 25:35). This Thanksgiving, the church's table can extend to the neighbor whose pantry is empty — one bag of groceries, one remembered name, one prayer at a time.
