AI Chatbots Keep Misquoting the Bible, and YouVersion's Chief Says That Must Change

By GLM Omnimedia News November 16, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

Bobby Gruenewald, the founder and chief executive of YouVersion, the nonprofit behind the Bible App, says the biggest problem facing his ministry is not getting people to open the Bible. It is getting the machines people now ask about the Bible to quote it correctly. The Bible App has been downloaded more than a billion times, and Gruenewald told Christianity Today that the leading artificial intelligence chatbots, including ChatGPT and Claude, regularly misquote Scripture.

Gruenewald spoke with Christianity Today in New York, where YouVersion unveiled a Times Square advertising campaign for Global Bible Month, a November effort to encourage readers to spend more time in the Word of God. He has spent more than a decade thinking about how technology and the church meet, and he said this new technology is different from every tool that came before it.

New data cited in the interview shows that half of the Christian questions put to ChatGPT are about interpreting the Bible. People treat the technology as though it were an oracle, Gruenewald said, because it speaks so fluently. Human beings have long associated fluency with intelligence, but a fluent answer can be greatly wrong, or right, or somewhere in between, and the person asking often cannot tell the difference unless they already know the answer. When the question concerns spiritual direction or the meaning of Scripture, he said, a wrong understanding is a serious matter.

YouVersion considered building a chatbot into the Bible App and even built a prototype about four or five years ago. It has not shipped one, for reasons Gruenewald described as centered on accuracy and safety. The technology is nondeterministic by design, which means its answers vary, and there is no simple fix when it answers wrong, the way a software bug can be fixed. Just as important, an answer that comes from the Bible App carries an implied authority in the minds of users. People may take what it says not only as a right answer, but as an answer carrying spiritual authority, as though it came from God himself. Gruenewald said that is not proper theology, but it is how people will receive it, and so the decision must be made with great care.

Safety is the other concern. Gruenewald said that every day, people in desperate situations download the app because they are searching for answers, and in some cases the app is their last resort. An open-ended chatbot asking, in effect, what the Bible says about any subject would invite people in the darkest moments of their lives to pour out their struggles to a machine. In his view, if a person's question turns toward suicide or another crisis, the chatbot should stop and the person should be directed to a human being, because a trained person is needed to walk with someone through such a situation.

YouVersion is now pressing the major artificial intelligence companies to do better. Gruenewald said the ministry has been in conversation with the frontier laboratories, challenging them to quote the Bible accurately. He called it shocking that a superintelligent technology cannot quote a known book correctly, and said a fair standard for ChatGPT, Anthropic, and Google Gemini is that sacred text be quoted correctly. YouVersion is working on a benchmark to measure how accurately the models quote Scripture, and it has offered, behind the scenes, to help the companies improve. The ministry has quoted the Bible accurately for more than eighteen years, he said, and every time, it is a direct quote.

The stakes, in his view, are generational. If the care that goes into translating the Bible correctly is slowly undone because translations are subtly changed a little at a time, then over a generation the text itself would be eroded, and Gruenewald said he feels a responsibility to steward the text faithfully. Misquoting the Bible, he said, becomes a very big problem if it is allowed to take root.

YouVersion does use artificial intelligence in the app, but in a different way. Its search, for example, can understand that a person asking what to say to someone who lost a father is looking for comfort in grief, and it returns actual Bible verses on the subject. What the person receives is not generated text, Gruenewald said, but a literal verse. The technology helps find the Scripture; it does not write it.

Gruenewald was clear that he is not against artificial intelligence. He encourages people to understand what the tool is and what the right job for it is, and he said the technology has sped up the ministry's engineering work dramatically. He also sees a hopeful trend in the midst of the confusion the technology brings: a generation that questions the truth of nearly everything it consumes is searching for something that is true. Interest in the Bible is rising, and print Bible sales are up, according to the interview. For a ministry whose only aim is to get people engaged in Scripture, Gruenewald said, those are encouraging signs.

The standard he sets is a simple one, and it is one the church has always held: when the Word of God is quoted, it must be quoted rightly. In an age when a machine can speak fluently about anything, the believer's task is unchanged, to open the Book itself and to test every word against what is written.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

The Lord Jesus prayed, "Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth" (John 17:17). When a machine speaks fluently about the Bible, fluency is not faithfulness. The believer must always go back to the Book itself, for only the Word of God, quoted rightly and received humbly, can sanctify, guide, and save.
