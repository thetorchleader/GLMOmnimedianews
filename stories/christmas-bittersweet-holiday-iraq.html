Christmas Is a Bittersweet Holiday for Iraq's Ancient Christians

By GLM Omnimedia News November 24, 2026 Source: International Christian Concern

BAGHDAD, Iraq — Christmas is a national holiday in Iraq. The government formally declared it so in December 2018, and every year since, December 25 has been a day off for all Iraqis, Christian and Muslim alike. For the Christians who remain in one of the oldest Christian lands on earth, the holiday carries a joy that is real — and a sorrow that is just as real.

“It feels like a token move to appear non-discriminatory,” said “Petros,” an Assyrian Christian now living in the United States, in an interview with International Christian Concern. He said the official celebration “ignores the native traditions” of the region’s Christians and “instead opts for a European-style Christmas” of Santa Claus and Christmas trees that do not naturally grow in the area.

Iraq was already celebrating the birth of Christ when most of Europe was still pagan. The faith arrived in the first century A.D., and the land that is now Iraq has been home to Christians ever since. Petros believes the government’s recognition of Christmas is more public relations than repentance — a way, in his view, to deflect attention from what Iraq’s Christians have suffered in recent years.

“Yonita,” an Assyrian woman who is Chaldean Catholic and also lives in the United States, sees a different motive. “Iraq is trying to rebuild itself,” she said, adding that the country may be “taking notes” from more successful neighbors that allow comparatively more religious freedom. She suspects the Christmas holiday is also meant to show camaraderie with the West — and to make the homeland seem more welcoming to the many Iraqi Christians living abroad, a community she described as quite well-off financially. “There was a formal call inviting us back to Iraq [to] invest in the rebuilding of the cradle of civilization,” she said.

Whatever the government’s true motive, one painful fact is hard to deny: by the time Christmas became a national holiday, most of Iraq’s Christians had already been driven from their homeland. Conditions over the previous fifteen years were so severe that the country lost up to 90 percent of its Christian community. Those who remain — most of them Chaldean Catholic or members of the Assyrian Church of the East — now make up less than half of 1 percent of Iraq’s population.

The decline began long before the holiday was declared. The Christian population had already shrunk considerably during the 24-year rule of former president Saddam Hussein. Hussein was no Islamic extremist, and his longtime deputy, Tariq Aziz, was himself a Christian, at least in name. Petros said his fellow Assyrian Christians “either liked Hussein for providing stability and jobs, or hated him for killing their family members.” Many wanted him to stay in power, he said, only because they feared the chaos that would follow his fall. “It’s like the saying goes,” Petros said. “Better to deal with the devil you know than the devil you don’t.”

The fall came in 2003, when Hussein was ousted from power, and with it came an era of rising Islamic extremism, most notably the Islamic State group, known as ISIS. Some Iraqi Christians were even accused of collaborating with the American military. As the danger grew, most Christians fled, judging the other choices before them — violent death, forced conversion, or paying a religious tax — to be unbearable. Those who stayed were often hunted.

The stories from those years are hard to read. In June 2007, in the northern city of Mosul, a priest and his assistants were killed right in front of his church, and for several hours afterward nobody dared retrieve their bodies, for fear that anyone who appeared on the scene would be killed next. In February 2008, Mosul’s Chaldean Catholic archbishop was seized from his car; his driver and bodyguards were killed at the scene, and the archbishop himself was never seen alive again. He had warned his people never to pay a ransom for him, fearing the money would only finance more evil.

Iraqi authorities tried to fight the extremists but ultimately failed to protect religious minorities. Petros believes a significant portion of Iraqis were content to let the extremists abuse and drive out religious minorities, and he named the Sunni Arab population and the Kurdish political class as the two groups he considers most consistently hostile toward Iraq’s Christians — and, in his view, the biggest threat to those who remain.

The threat has not ended with the defeat of ISIS. Though the group’s menace in Iraq has diminished, much of the country remains exposed to various militia groups, including Shia militias backed by neighboring Iran. The U.S. Department of State has said the number of Iraqi Muslims who have converted to Christianity is unknown; a separate report claims that more than 5,000 have done so since the early 2000s. Petros said he has never met an Iraqi Muslim convert to Christianity, and their situation, he said, would be fragile indeed. Iraqi Christians have also reported growing difficulty finding good work, and the confiscation of their land and homes.

Yet Petros has not given up hope. Despite all the bloodshed and the much-reduced state of Iraqi Christianity, he believes there remains a chance at a sustainable future for Christians in Iraq — but only, he said, if it “requires a serious effort” from the government as well as from Iraqi Christians both in the homeland and in the diaspora. This Christmas, the ancient church of Iraq will light its candles again, in a land where the Gospel was preached before most of the world had ever heard the name of Jesus.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

Scripture tells us to “remember them that are in bonds, as bound with them” (Hebrews 13:3), and Jesus said, “Blessed are they which are persecuted for righteousness’ sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven” (Matthew 5:10). The Christians of Iraq kept the faith in the land where it first took root, and they kept it at a terrible cost. This Christmas, while we sing carols in safety, let us remember them, pray for them, and stand with them — for they are our brothers and sisters, and their hope is our hope.
