Is Plagiarism a Sin? Christian Writer Says the Debate Reveals a Deeper Problem

By GLM Omnimedia News November 27, 2026 Source: Protestia

A Christian writer is asking a question that has stirred sharp debate across evangelical social media: is plagiarism actually a sin? His answer — no, not in itself — has drawn both applause and strong pushback, and the argument behind it reaches well beyond one man's opinion into how Christian scholars, pastors, and teachers think about truth, credit, and humility.

David Morrill, writing for Protestia on November 25, said the uproar began when he stated on social media that plagiarism is neither a biblically defined moral offense nor an independently actionable legal one. Even after he explained that he meant plagiarism can involve sin without itself being a sin, the objections kept coming. Critics told him plagiarism is lying. Others told him it is stealing.

Morrill answered that the comparison misses the point. Plagiarism, he argued, is to lying what killing is to murder: a broad category of conduct within which a specific moral transgression can occur. Showing that two people said essentially the same thing, he wrote, does not establish that either of them has sinned.

What caught his attention, he said, was who was most upset. Many of the strongest objectors work professionally in Christian writing, publishing, apologetics, and theological discourse. That observation led him to a longer argument, published in fuller form in the Reformation Journal, about what he calls the identity problem of the evangelical intelligentsia.

His case runs like this. A surgeon's distinction is obvious: he can remove an appendix and the man in the next pew cannot. But a theologian's subject matter — biblical truth — belongs to every believer just as much as to the scholar, and every Christian is obligated to test teaching against Scripture. So how does the professional theologian maintain his professional distinction? Morrill's answer is the academic pecking order: citations, credentials, institutional affiliations, and recognition function as a kind of currency. The more established a scholar is, the more likely others are to cite him, and the more he is cited, the more established he becomes. Morrill calls this "ideological tenure."

Once a scholar's name becomes attached to an idea, he argued, later expressions of that idea are expected to include a small payment of intellectual tribute — whether or not the later writer actually took the idea from him. In that light, the claim that plagiarism is "stealing" starts to make sense, Morrill wrote: the scholar has not necessarily been deprived of property, but of the currency of his profession — recognition.

Generative artificial intelligence, he added, has made the question sharper. A 2023 study published in Science found that AI substantially improved professional writing, with the biggest gains going to people who were not strong writers to begin with. The person with great ideas and mediocre writing skills can now compete with the person with mediocre ideas and great writing skills. If polished prose is no longer a scarce distinction, Morrill argued, the credentialed class has all the more reason to guard the distinctions it still holds — and what better way, for Christian intellectuals, than to make observing them a matter of Christian obedience?

Morrill was careful to say he is not accusing every critic of conscious self-interest, and he is not arguing against citation standards where they belong. In a classroom, a student who copies another's work and presents it as his own has cheated, because he claimed to have performed a task he did not perform. But, he wrote, "the rest of us aren't turning in a term paper every time we communicate an idea." Teaching one's children something learned from someone else is not plagiarism in any morally meaningful sense, he said — it is learning, which is what teaching is for.

He pointed to the apostle Paul's instruction to Timothy to entrust what he had learned to faithful men who would teach others also (2 Timothy 2:2). The goal, Morrill wrote, was not to establish a perpetual stream of intellectual royalties payable to Paul. "By all means, give credit where credit is actually due," he wrote. "Be honest about your work. Acknowledge the people who have helped you, cite your sources when doing so benefits your readers, and don't pretend to have done things you haven't done."

But, he concluded, if one Christian is going to accuse another of sin, the charge needs more than two screenshots with matching words circled. "If he lied, demonstrate the lie. If he stole, identify what he stole. If he cheated, explain what obligation he dishonestly evaded." The academy may have its conventions, he wrote, "but it doesn't have its own Sinai."

Not everyone agrees. Readers responding to the piece argued that plagiarism is by definition passing off another's work as one's own, and that doing so is a form of lying and cheating whatever the setting. Others pointed out that Scripture itself often quotes the Old Testament without naming the source, and that Jesus and the apostles routinely repeated words first spoken by others — suggesting the question is more complicated than either side's slogans allow. The debate comes amid the wider controversy over apologist Wesley Huff, who has publicly admitted that papers he posted online nearly a decade ago used the work of other scholars without proper credit.

For now, Morrill's essay stands as a challenge to the Christian world to be precise about what it calls sin — and honest about whose interests a charge of sin might serve.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

<p>The Bible calls God's people to honesty without partiality: "Provide things honest in the sight of all men" (Romans 12:17). Give credit where credit is due, tell the truth about your work, and do not claim as your own what another labored to produce — but be just as careful not to call sin what God has not called sin. "God hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise" (1 Corinthians 1:27), and no man's name, degree, or reputation owns the truth of God's Word, which belongs to all who will receive it.</p>
