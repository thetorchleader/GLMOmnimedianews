Nigerian Christians Wrestle With a Surrogacy Boom Driven by Poverty and Demand From Abroad

By GLM Omnimedia News November 16, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

LAGOS, Nigeria — Marvy owned a small men's clothing store in Lagos State, a business she started with a loan. Then a fire in the fall of 2019 burned everything to ashes, and she spent the next five years working odd jobs to repay what she owed.

"I did all kinds of jobs, but there was no stable income," she said. "I became depressed."

Last May, a friend suggested she consider becoming a surrogate mother. The friend told her many other Nigerian women were doing it for extra cash. Christianity Today is using only Marvy's nickname to protect her privacy. She went looking for agents on social media and contacted at least three. The pay they offered was small — which, she learned, is normal for surrogacy in Nigeria. She was discouraged by the offers, and troubled by the idea of a pregnancy arranged as a market transaction. But she still needed the money.

Last month, one of the agents messaged her again on Instagram. "I told them that I would get back to them," she said. She thinks about the offer every morning and is still weighing what it would mean for her.

Marvy's story is far from unusual. Britain's Children and Family Court Advisory and Support Service, known as Cafcass, reported this year that Nigeria has become the most popular destination after the United States for British couples seeking a surrogate mother abroad. Cafcass estimates there has been an almost tenfold increase over the past decade in British couples pursuing surrogacy in the West African country.

Supporters of surrogacy say the arrangement helps couples struggling with infertility to have children. But Christian organizations and other groups in Nigeria have pushed back against its growing use, saying it exploits women and degrades the female body.

Poverty in Nigeria has worsened over the past three years, and about 140 million Nigerians now live below the national poverty line, according to the report. Most Nigerians are young and lack economic opportunities, and many women who agree to carry a child do so to earn quick money. Meanwhile, the couples seeking surrogates often live in wealthy Western countries such as the United Kingdom, the United States, or Canada. Many of them, family lawyers say, have family ties in Nigeria. Surrogate mothers in Nigeria typically earn between 2 million and 7 million naira — roughly $1,500 to $5,200 — for a pregnancy, while the agents receive more from the couples. "The agents receive more than that from the couples, but the mothers only get a little," Marvy said. "You are almost being used."

The global surrogacy industry is valued at $22.4 billion and is expected to reach $202 billion by 2034. Evangelicals have generally criticized the growing use of assisted reproduction, though some disagreement remains about the ethics of the practice, which usually involves in vitro fertilization. Objections have also come from beyond the church: Reem Alsalem, a United Nations special rapporteur, said last year that surrogacy was predatory and should be seen as a form of violence, exploitation, and abuse against women and girls, particularly those who are impoverished. In 2021, the African media organization The Elephant reported that commercial surrogacy agencies were exploiting, intimidating, and sometimes trafficking women in Kenya so they could carry children. One surrogate told the outlet that an agency forced her into a late-term abortion after a commissioning parent changed their mind, and that she was never fully compensated.

"They know that poor women are often defenseless and don't have many options," said Maria-Goretti Ogu, a pro-life advocate in Lagos State. "But a woman's womb is not for rent. It's not for commercial benefits." Ogu works on anti-surrogacy advocacy through her local Catholic church's pro-life group, which also focuses on opposing abortion and championing adoption. She believes that if more Nigerians understood how surrogacy works, opposition to it would grow. But she said only a few churches are committed to teaching their congregations about the ethical concerns, and many churches avoid sensitive topics, leaving a vacuum. "Most girls are now being influenced by what they see and read online," she said.

Ebenezer Obiomah, pastor of Grace Community Bible Church in the city of Jos, agrees. He has counseled Christian young people who want to know whether the Bible gives ethical answers on the subject. He tells them to examine God's clear commands and points out that surrogacy usually turns both the surrogate mother and the child into commodities. "Surrogacy should be a no ... for Christians," Obiomah said. At the same time, he said churches should show compassion to couples struggling with fertility who consider surrogacy. "Their desire to have children is good," he said. "We must not dismiss it."

Nigeria has no established law governing IVF or surrogacy, and that legal gap has allowed the practice to flourish. South Africa is the only African country with strict requirements: it permits only altruistic surrogacy, in which a surrogate cannot be paid beyond reimbursement for medical and other pregnancy costs, and a high court must approve the agreement before a pregnancy begins. In Nigeria, Ghana, and Kenya, surrogacy operates under unclear laws and court agreements. Nigeria's House of Representatives began debating a five-part bill last year to prohibit commercial surrogacy and regulate noncommercial arrangements. The bill's sponsor has argued it would protect the rights of surrogate mothers, including informed consent, medical care, and reimbursement for expenses. But the legislation has faced opposition from a pro-life group, which has slowed its progress. Philip Njemanze, chairman of the Global Prolife Alliance, said his organization opposed the bill because it viewed the limits on commercial surrogacy as a "clever tactic" to shift focus from legalizing altruistic surrogacy. His organization favors a national ban on both IVF and surrogacy, and Njemanze said he has worked with non-Christian groups, including Muslim organizations, to resist the bill.

Marvy, meanwhile, is still deciding. A few days after her friend first encouraged her to consider surrogacy, she learned that another young woman in her friend group had been a surrogate twice. Yet her own nondenominational church has never addressed the topic, and she is afraid even to raise the question. The agent who messaged her is still waiting for her answer. "I won't encourage any girl to be a surrogate. It is my situation that has pushed me to consider it," she said. "I still don't know whether to take the offer or reject it."

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

A child's life is a gift from God, never a product to be bought, and a woman's body is never a thing to be rented. Scripture calls children "a heritage from the LORD" (Psalm 127:3) and commands God's people to "speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves" and defend the rights of the poor and needy (Proverbs 31:8-9). The Church must name exploitation plainly, teach the truth about life with courage, and surround struggling families — both the poor mother and the childless couple — with compassion, prayer, and practical help.
