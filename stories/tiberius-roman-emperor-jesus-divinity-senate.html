Did Emperor Tiberius Try to Declare Jesus God? Ancient Writings Reopen the Question

By GLM Omnimedia News November 10, 2026 Source: Charisma News

The Roman Empire sentenced Jesus Christ to death on a cross. Yet within a few short years of the crucifixion, the emperor of that same empire may have asked his own Senate to recognize Jesus as God.

That striking possibility is drawing fresh attention through new research by Luigi Zito, published in Aequitas Magazine. Zito examined ancient writings that describe Emperor Tiberius bringing the question of Jesus' divinity before the Roman Senate around A.D. 35. According to the accounts, the Senate refused. But the claim itself, if it happened, would mean word of Christ reached the highest seat of Roman power far sooner than many people imagine.

The story traces back to Tertullian, an early Christian writer who lived about a century and a half after these events. In his Apology, Tertullian wrote that Tiberius received reports from Palestine about Jesus and then supported recognizing Him within Rome's religious system. The Senate reportedly said no. Tertullian further claimed that Tiberius did not drop the matter there, but kept his favorable view of Jesus and threatened punishment for those who brought accusations against Christians.

The timeline fits what history records about the men involved. Tiberius was emperor while Pontius Pilate governed Judea, which means he held the throne at the time of Jesus' crucifixion. Zito also looked at the possibility that Pilate himself sent official reports about Jesus to Rome, which would explain how the emperor learned of Him so quickly.

It is important to be plain about what this is and what it is not. This is not a newly discovered Senate record or an official Roman document. It is a claim preserved in an early Christian writing, now being examined again by a modern researcher. Historians differ over how much weight to give Tertullian's account, and the question of whether Tiberius truly acted as described remains open. The evidence invites interest, not certainty.

Zito himself offered a sober note about the emperor's possible motives. Tiberius may not have been drawn to Jesus out of faith at all. Zito suggested the emperor could have seen recognizing Christ as a political move, a way to keep peace in a region already full of religious tension. An emperor's interest in a new faith is not the same as an emperor's belief in it.

Still, the account points to something the early church understood from the beginning. The message the apostles carried was not about another political leader or a new philosophy. It was that Jesus Christ, condemned to die under Roman authority, had conquered death and risen from the grave. That message did not stay in Jerusalem. Within years, according to these ancient writings, it had reached the ears of the emperor himself.

Whether or not the Senate ever voted on the divinity of Christ, the church's testimony has never depended on any government's approval. Rome possessed the power to nail Jesus to a cross, but it could not keep the news of His resurrection confined to one small province. The Gospel went out, as Jesus promised, from Jerusalem to the ends of the earth.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

<p>No Senate ever voted Jesus into godhood, and none ever could. Scripture declares that God has already highly exalted Him and given Him a name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow (Philippians 2:9-10). Rome could crucify the Lord of glory, but it could not vote on who He is. His divinity rests on the empty tomb, not on the approval of any earthly power.</p>
