South Korean Churches Reshape Worship to Reach a New Generation

By GLM Omnimedia News October 23, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

On a narrow street in Seoul's Yeongdeungpo District sits a glass-fronted building that looks like an ordinary café. A neon-lit staircase descends to a dim performance space where lyrics flash across a large screen as driving music fills the room. Young ministers and attendees move freely between the stage and the seats — jumping, dancing, and singing. The boundary between performer and congregant all but disappears.

A pastor in a T-shirt stands at the front, making eye contact with his audience as he speaks. His message on the importance of community in a Christian's life unfolds more like a conversation than a monologue. There are no visible crosses or church signage. This is a Sunday service at Knock Church, one of a growing number of South Korean congregations rethinking how church is done in order to reach the next generation, according to reporting by Christianity Today.

The experiment stands in sharp contrast to traditional Korean churches, where a service leader guides the liturgy from the pulpit and a formally dressed pastor delivers a sermon to a seated audience, with recitations of the Apostles' Creed and the Lord's Prayer following a fixed order. That structure served Korea's remarkable era of church growth — Christians grew from 18 percent of the population in 1970 to 30 percent in 2000, and South Korea became the world's second-largest missionary-sending nation, home to the planet's largest megachurch, Yoido Full Gospel Church. But recent surveys point to a troubling gap in passing the faith to younger generations.

A 2026 report by the Pastoral Data Research Institute found that 26 percent of university students identify as believers but do not attend church. Among teenagers who still attend, 41 percent have considered leaving. And 74 percent of those who have already left said they had no one with whom to share their struggles at church. The most cited reason for leaving was that worship services and sermons felt, in their words, "boring and meaningless."

Park Chan-yeol, the pastor of Knock Church, does not believe the youth exodus comes from a lack of personal faith. Having grown up in a large Pentecostal church, he has watched young people struggle to adapt to traditional structures. He points to what he calls a "prohibition-centered faith" — churches that have focused too heavily on what believers must not do, while failing to explain why belief matters in the first place. "The church should help young people discover and develop a faith of their own," he told Christianity Today.

At Knock Church, that philosophy shows up in small groups the church calls "crews." Bible study and prayer remain central, but members also join running groups and soccer teams throughout the week. The pastor emphasizes that a faith confined to Sunday attendance alone is unsustainable; instead, members share life together and offer every part of their lives to God. Park Jiwon, a 30-year-old attendee, said she came because she found mentors there — something she never had at her previous, much larger church, where people were divided by age group and she built no meaningful relationships with older believers.

Across town in Hongdae, Seoul's nightlife district, Newsong Church began in 2013 in a rented basement club, holding services among bars and entertainment venues. The message, said pastor Nam Bin, was that faith can exist in the heart of secular culture. The church has since grown enough to move into a larger facility near its original location. Newsong lowers barriers to entry by welcoming people regardless of their lifestyle, focusing first on helping them encounter the gospel. "It's important to create a structure where young people can ask their own questions and find answers for themselves," Nam said.

The pastors also point to the crushing societal pressures young Koreans face. In a fiercely competitive environment, young people pour most of their energy into academic achievement, exam preparation, and job hunting — youth unemployment remains above the global average. As responsibilities mount, sustaining church involvement becomes difficult. Both Knock and Newsong responded by embracing the digital world young people already inhabit: Sunday services run in person and via livestream, and early-morning prayer meetings and small groups are available online too. Newsong goes further, placing small groups and discipleship at the center of its ministry and empowering lay leaders to care for members rather than relying on staff.

The shift extends beyond individual congregations. This October, the youth gathering G2A (Go To All) brought roughly 14,500 young people together for a 10-hour worship event at KINTEX, a convention center just outside Seoul. At its inaugural gathering last year, participants came from some 3,000 churches nationwide, and about 80 percent were in their teens or 20s — a figure Christianity Today calls unprecedented in the Korean context. "The spark is already there," Nam said. "Now it needs to grow — into a river, and eventually into the sea."

Nam believes the movement is rooted in the Great Commission, where Jesus calls His followers to "make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19–20) — not merely to increase numbers, but to help faith become a living reality in each person's life. "It's not about form, but essence; not structure, but relationship; not rules, but the gospel," he said.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

The Apostle Paul wrote, "I have become all things to all people so that by all possible means I might save some" (1 Corinthians 9:22). Methods may change from generation to generation, but the message never does. As Moses charged Israel to impress God's commandments on their children "when you sit at home and when you walk along the road" (Deuteronomy 6:7), these Korean pastors are simply finding new roads on which the same ancient faith can be carried to the young.
