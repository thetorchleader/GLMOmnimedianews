Over Chai and Worship Songs, Young Christians in India Are Bringing Their Faith Into the Open

By GLM Omnimedia News November 12, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

In the southern Indian city of Hyderabad, a young man leans against a tree trunk and strums a worship song on his guitar while friends tap their feet to the music and others share plates of biryani nearby. Under warm halogen lights, a group from a local church spends the evening at Chirag, the city’s newest Christian café. Since opening its doors in May, Chirag — which means “light” in Urdu — has hosted informal conversations about life, purpose, meaning, and faith over chai, sandwiches, and good food, according to Christianity Today.

The café is the vision of Samuel Abhishek and his wife, Maureen Keerthana, who married in August 2025 and have worked on the project together ever since. Abhishek, who once dreamed of serving in God’s kingdom work without becoming a pastor or evangelist, spent years in his church’s youth development and community outreach before concluding that a Christian cultural center could serve his city. “More than ever, it is time we mainstream Christian culture,” he told Christianity Today. “Christian contribution to India’s cultural, social, and political life has been immense, yet the community has been inward looking. It is time we break from it.”

For many Indian Christians, that inward turn was a matter of necessity. Christians make up about 2.3 percent of India’s population, according to the most recent census in 2011, and most have long kept their faith inside their churches and homes in a Hindu-majority nation. A younger generation, however, is more open about displaying its faith in the public square, and the cafés are one expression of that change.

One regular at Chirag is 28-year-old Praharsha Deep, a corporate executive at Goldman Sachs by day who spends his evenings making music with his bandmates John Reynolds, Kevin Joseph, and Aaron Pranay — all of whom hold corporate jobs and worship at different churches. They meet at the café, which is attached to the Chirag Christian Cultural Center, for jam sessions, worship nights, and open mics. “You can’t go to every church in the town to connect with Christian youth there,” Deep said. “What makes Chirag an instant hit is that it enables youth to build godly bonds beyond churches and around common interests in an informal atmosphere.”

About 25 kilometers away, in Hyderabad’s commercial district, another Christian café serves South Indian and Western food amid gleaming high-rises, with live worship music, open mics, movie nights, board games, and art events centered on biblical themes. Christianity Today agreed not to name the café or its owners after they faced backlash from earlier media coverage. The couple who opened it also left corporate jobs, invested their savings, and borrowed money to build the space. Friends advised them not to link the café to their faith, warning it could hurt the business. Instead, they held to the promise of Matthew 6:33: “Seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you.”

The couple spent nearly three years researching the idea and visiting more than 200 cafés before opening their own. Kneeling in their empty concrete-and-steel storefront, they prayed for guidance and said they received a vision of building the café out of wood. Though the premium wood pushed them over budget, they worked with carpenters to finish the design in 37 days. “I feel that this is not [us] who did this, because God was behind every piece of wood,” the female co-owner said. “What we did here, it was just God, and I believe Jesus was [the] carpenter.” Hundreds of visitors, they said, have told them there is “so much peace here” amid the stress of the surrounding corporate offices.

In the neighboring city of Bengaluru, a café called Chai 3:16 has been serving students since 2015. Its name carries a double meaning: chai means “life” in Hebrew, and 3:16 points to the beloved passage in John’s Gospel. Founded by gospel musician Benny Prasad, the café was begun as a way to combat youth suicide and loneliness. Bengaluru, known for its booming technology sector, has the highest suicide rates among Indian metropolitan areas; in 2024, 2,430 people there died by suicide.

At Chai 3:16, trained biblical counselors work as waiters. Customers decide how much to pay for their drinks — or whether to pay at all. “The goal is to sit down and build relationships with students,” Prasad said in a recorded interview. “Counselors listen until the students finish, and then we share the love of God and speak about truth and life.” Prasad knows the darkness firsthand: at 16, struggling with failing health and poor grades, he contemplated suicide before an encounter with Jesus at a youth camp transformed his life. He has since shared his testimony around the world as a gospel musician.

Volunteers at Chai 3:16 come from around the world, must be fluent in English, and agree not to smoke or drink alcohol while serving. They receive food and lodging free of charge, though the work is unpaid and can last from one week to one year. The café also trains its volunteers in discipleship, worship music, and apologetics, and holds live music on Fridays, movie showings on Saturdays, and church services on Sundays.

These cafés reflect a desire among Indian Christians to own their faith openly, even as they face growing pressure under the country’s Hindu nationalist government. “It is a democratic country, and our constitution offers us freedom of speech and religion,” Abhishek said. “We want our café to act as a springboard for Christians to rediscover their community’s contribution to India and once again participate widely in its social, cultural, and political life.”

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

“Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven” (Matthew 5:16). These young believers in India are not hiding their lamp under a bushel — they are setting it on a table, over a cup of chai, where a hurting neighbor can see it. Pray for the Christians of India, that their open witness would draw many to Christ and that their freedom to speak and worship would be protected.
