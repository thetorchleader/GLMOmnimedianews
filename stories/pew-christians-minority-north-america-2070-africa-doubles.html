Pew: By 2070, Most of the World's Christians Will Live in Sub-Saharan Africa

By GLM Omnimedia News November 19, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

A new report from the Pew Research Center projects a very different map of world Christianity by the year 2070. Christians will become a minority in North America, the report says, while the Christian population of sub-Saharan Africa will more than double.

Pew's report, titled "The Future of World Religions," looks ahead fifty years by studying recent surveys and tracing three forces that shape religious identity: birth rates, migration around the world, and religious switching, when people leave the faith of their childhood for another religion or for no religion at all.

By 2070, Christians will make up 46 percent of the population of North America, according to Pew, and 52 percent of Europe's population. Europe's slightly higher share is partly the result of migration, though some European countries, including Spain and Germany, are expected to see a steeper decline.

Meanwhile, the Christian population of sub-Saharan Africa will grow to 1.6 billion people, more than double its size today, and will account for 54 percent of all Christians in the world. By 2070, the Democratic Republic of the Congo will have passed the United States as the country with the largest number of Christians.

"In the United States, we have seen over the last few decades a decline in the Christian share," Conrad Hackett, associate director of research at Pew, said at a news conference. "But we're projecting that globally there will be an increase in the number of Christians ... with growth in sub-Saharan Africa offsetting declines in Europe and in North America."

Pew says the rise of Christianity in sub-Saharan Africa is all but certain, driven mostly by birth rates. The decline in the United States and other Western nations is driven mostly by religious switching, as people raised Christian leave the church.

"The global change in what one might think of as the center of gravity in Christianity is interesting," said Alan Cooperman, director of religion research at Pew. "You take the trends we see now and take them all the way out to 2070, and the distribution of Christians around the world looks very different from the way it does today."

Signs of this shift are already appearing. African churches long tied to large Western denominations are seeking more independence. The Catholic Church, the Anglican Communion, and the United Methodist Church have all wrestled in recent years with disagreements between African members and church leadership in the United States or Britain, often over questions of sexuality and the role of women. Within the Anglican Church, an alliance of African churches took a major step this year toward independence from the Archbishop of Canterbury and is considering whether to break those ties fully and appoint a rival leader.

The flow of missions is changing as well. Western giving and Western missionaries to Africa have declined; last year the Presbyterian Church (USA) closed its foreign mission board, while the Southern Baptist Convention recently voted to give its International Mission Board a larger share of the denomination's budget. At the same time, a growing number of missionaries now come from non-Western countries such as Brazil and South Korea.

This is a relatively new kind of study for Pew. Four years ago, the center released its first major projections for religious identity in the United States through 2070, finding that the share of American adults who are Christian would fall below half, largely because of religious switching. Last year Pew reported signs that the share of nonreligious Americans may be leveling off, but the center says that small shift does little to change the fifty-year trajectory.

Even if religious switching stopped entirely, Pew says, the Christian share of the American population would still decline by 2070. "If we were to (hypothetically) shut off the spigot of religious switching, which by and large has brought people from Christianity into the unaffiliated category ... if we were to say, 'No more of that' all the way until [2070], we would still project a decline in the share of Christians," Cooperman said.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

The center of gravity of the Christian faith is moving, but the promise has not changed. "All the ends of the world shall remember and turn unto the LORD: and all the kindreds of the nations shall worship before thee" (Psalm 22:27). Wherever the church grows, the call is the same: preach the Gospel, make disciples, and pray for the brothers and sisters God is raising up in every nation.
