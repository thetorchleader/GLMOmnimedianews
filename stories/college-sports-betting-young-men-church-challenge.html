An $11,000 Parlay Win and a Quiet Warning: Sports Betting's Grip on Young Men

By GLM Omnimedia News October 30, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

On a college-football Saturday in Texas, a student named Ryan Cass watched a 15-part parlay come down to a single game. Fourteen legs had already hit. From a tailgate outside Texas A&M's Kyle Field, then from the stands, then from a sports bar near campus, he followed the final game as word of his wager spread through the room. When Arizona held its opponent at the one-yard line, the bar fell silent — and then Cass learned he had won $11,000.

For millions of young American men, that kind of win is the dream. Since the Supreme Court ruled in 2018 that sports gambling's legality should be left to the states, betting apps have spread across the country, putting a sportsbook in nearly every pocket. According to reporting by Christianity Today, an estimated 27 percent of Americans now hold active sports betting accounts — and among men ages 18 to 49, the figure rises to 52 percent.

The betting has moved well beyond sports. Through prediction markets such as Kalshi and Polymarket, people can now wager on Oscar winners, papal conclaves, political campaigns, and even military operations. Christianity Today notes that by June 2026, more than a billion dollars had been wagered this year on war operations alone, including by military personnel, and that lawmakers have recently called for a ban on betting on wildfires over fears it could incentivize arson.

For campus ministries, the trend presents a pastoral challenge. Betting is a pastime Christians have historically understood as a vice, yet many young men no longer see it as wrong at all. Ben Weber, a Campus Outreach worker at colleges in Alabama, likened the explosion of sports betting to the spread of pornography: once confined to Las Vegas and tribal casinos, it is now everywhere, instantly available on a phone.

Griffin Oakley, a Gen Z seminarian from Nashville who began betting as soon as he turned 21, sees it differently. For him, betting is a social activity — a group chat that keeps him in touch with friends from home. Stuart Gunner, the director of Campus Outreach at Ole Miss, reports a similar picture on campus: walk into any fraternity house, he says, and sports will be on the television and at least one young man will have money on the game, with no sense of guilt or shame surrounding it.

That lack of stigma is what worries ministers most. Gunner observes that the damage of gambling is easy to see in a casino, where an older man sits at a roulette table in the middle of the day trying to win back what he has lost. With college students, he says, the harm is harder to spot because they are only at the beginning of an addiction rather than at the end of one.

The numbers bear out the concern. Gambling disorder diagnoses among 18-to-29-year-olds have doubled in the eight years since the Supreme Court decision, according to research cited by Christianity Today. Cass himself, after his $11,000 win, ended up $15,000 in credit card debt and took a break from gambling — but he has since returned, saying the social pull is what brought him back.

Yet the social pull is real, and ministers say the Church cannot ignore it. Young men describe betting together as a way of belonging in the midst of a loneliness crisis — sweating out a wager side by side, the way friends once simply watched a game together. Gunner is encouraging his students to test whether they can put the phone down for two weeks and still enjoy the games with their friends, without feeling pressure to put money on them. The stadium, the fraternity living room, and the sports bar will still be there, he notes — winning parlay or not.

The question for the Church is not only whether betting is wrong, but what young men are truly hungry for when they bet: friendship, excitement, and a stake in something bigger than themselves. Those are longings God made, and they find their truest home not in a wager but in a community built on something that cannot be lost on a final play.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

The hunger behind the bet is real: young men long for friendship, excitement, and belonging. But Scripture warns that the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil (1 Timothy 6:10), and that true contentment is found in Christ, not in chance. The Church's answer is not merely to condemn the wager, but to offer the deeper community young men are actually seeking — one where they are known, welcomed, and not measured by what they win or lose. "For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" (Matthew 6:21, KJV).
