Christianity Today Faces a $3 Million Deficit as a 70-Year-Old Ministry Fights for Its Future

By GLM Omnimedia News November 25, 2026 Source: Protestia

Christianity Today, the 70-year-old magazine long regarded as a flagship publication of American evangelicalism, expects to finish 2026 more than $3 million in the red, according to an internal financial report obtained by the Roys Report. The publication, which describes itself as fighting for its very survival, is now working to balance its budget by 2027.

The report, covered in an exclusive article by the Roys Report, shows revenue from subscriptions, advertising, and product sales running $80,000 to $90,000 below monthly benchmarks. Ambitious plans to grow revenue by 15 percent a year have not come to pass, and several large projects have failed to pay for themselves.

Among them is the magazine's new smartphone app, which cost more than $200,000 to develop but has brought in only about 100 new subscriptions, according to current and former staff members with detailed knowledge of internal discussions. Its best-known podcast, "The Rise and Fall of Mars Hill," was downloaded 2.5 million times in 2021 but made no money, the Roys Report found, after advertising deals fell through amid delays and poor communication.

Another podcast, "This Great & Complicated Place," an eight-episode series released in December 2024 about race, cities, and the church, drew so few listeners that it ended up costing the magazine about $250 per download, according to the report.

The financial strain has already brought deep cuts. Christianity Today has gone through multiple rounds of layoffs in 2025 and in recent months, letting go of 40 percent of its editorial team and 20 percent of its overall staff, with some layoffs prompting further resignations. The magazine has also named Erik Petrik as its new editor-in-chief, its sixth editor-in-chief in seven years.

A technology failure made matters worse. A change to new accounting software resulted in the loss of credit card data, which disrupted renewals and cost the magazine about 500 subscribers a month for nearly two years — 23,000 paying members in all. The publication has lost more than 12,000 paid subscribers since 2020, through attrition during the pandemic and the software failure that followed.

Grants and donations have kept the magazine afloat. It received $5 million from the Lilly Endowment in 2025 for its "Next Generation Storytelling Project," and more than $9 million in grants from faith-based and secular agencies in 2023 alone. An internal report notes that in 2024, the magazine received gifts from 2,256 givers, including 363 sustaining partners giving $1,000 or more and 66 major givers giving $5,000 or more. The team raised $8.6 million in 2023 and $10 million in 2024. In 2027, it expects a $4 million payout from Gloo, the Christian technology company.

Even so, balancing the budget in 2027 will require raising an extra $1 million in donations plus another $1 million in other revenue, according to the internal report. One fundraising effort, a "Billy Graham legacy fund," was announced and then abandoned for lack of interest, a former vice president told the Roys Report.

One of the few parts of the operation that makes money is the podcast hosted by Russell Moore — but Moore is leaving Christianity Today after Thanksgiving, the Roys Report noted.

The magazine has also struggled to turn a growing global audience into income. As of 2025, 38 percent of its readership and 16 percent of its subscribers come from outside the United States, but at an annual subscription price of $72, monetizing those readers has proved difficult. "We got to a point where one-third of all readers were outside the United States," former global managing editor Morgan Lee told the Roys Report. "We struggled to get the digital infrastructure that would create a pathway to monetize those readers."

Christianity Today was founded in 1956 by evangelist Billy Graham as a magazine to give voice to evangelical Christianity. Its leaders now face the hard question of how a storied institution adapts when the economics that sustained it for seven decades no longer hold.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

Every ministry must count the cost before it builds. "For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?" (Luke 14:28). The Lord calls His people to faithful stewardship of what He entrusts to them, and to trust Him — not grants, apps, or audiences — for the work to endure.
