Cast Out of Christian Music, Now Speaking Out: A New Film Revisits the 1990s CCM Era

By GLM Omnimedia News November 26, 2026 Source: Religion News Service

In the early 2000s, Michael Passons stood at the top of the Contemporary Christian Music world. His vocal group Avalon had earned more than a dozen Christian number-one songs and had even broken into the mainstream, taking home an American Music Award in 2003. “We were sitting right up there with Britney and Justin,” Passons recalled in a new report.

Then, he says, it all fell apart. Not long after that award, Passons says his bandmates told him he was out of the group. The reason, as he tells it, was the suspicion that he was gay. The group told a magazine he had chosen to leave, replaced him quickly, and had him sign a nondisclosure agreement.

Passons is now telling his side of the story in a new documentary, “Safe For the Whole Family,” which premiered at the Nashville Film Festival in September. The film, directed by Jason Ikeler, turns a critical eye on the peak era of Christian pop in the 1990s and the people the industry left behind.

One of the former Avalon members involved in Passons’s departure has backed up his account. Melissa Greene told NPR in an email, “What Michael describes is what happened. I was in the room, and I take ownership of my part in it. We were inside a system that taught us his attraction was a danger to the ministry. I didn’t think I was only believing a system. I thought I was believing God.” Greene said she later realized she was wrong and asked Passons for forgiveness. The other two Avalon members involved did not respond to NPR’s request for comment.

Passons says the fallout went far beyond losing a job in a band. He describes what amounted to a blacklisting, saying he could not even find work as a church music minister afterward. But when Ikeler approached him about the film, he was ready to speak. “We were all coming together as a voice for what it felt like to have our voices silenced,” he said.

The documentary features other artists with painful stories of their own. Singer Nikki Leonti signed her first record deal at age 13 and says she was held up as a model of modesty and purity, with a public commitment to remain abstinent until marriage. But as she tells it in the film, the people handling her career failed to protect her from being pressured into sex against her will. The man who got her pregnant was in a Christian band, she says, yet she alone was shamed by the industry and made to beg its forgiveness.

“There is a lot of contradiction and hypocrisy when it comes to who gets punished and who does not,” Leonti reflects in the film. She says she spent years on what she calls an “apology tour” before deciding, after therapy and years of sharing her experiences on podcasts, to take part in the documentary.

The film’s third central figure is singer-songwriter Jennifer Knapp, who was unusual among Christian stars of the era in that she was not raised in the church. From the start of her career, she questioned what the industry asked her to represent, and she flatly refused to promote the “True Love Waits” abstinence campaign, calling it “a marketing package of shame.”

Ikeler, the filmmaker, grew up listening to these singers. He is gay, and he says he found himself alienated from evangelical Christianity and wanted to make sense of the subculture he had experienced. He began reaching out to his old Christian pop heroes in 2023, and he says he reassured them the film would not be a takedown of the faith itself. “I don’t wanna make a super scathing thing. I want to tell the truth. I don’t wanna sweep anything under the rug,” he said.

The film raises hard questions for the church and for Christian music. Passons puts it plainly: “The Christian music business is a business. And in that culture, being a gay person was bad for business. So, everyone was gonna lose money.” Ikeler adds that the contradiction runs deep: “I think the grossest part of it is you’re making content that’s supposed to be safe for kids, and look what you’re letting happen to these artists that you’re making money off of.”

It is worth noting what the film is and is not. It is the artists’ own accounts of what they experienced, as told to NPR and to the camera — not a court finding. No charges are reported in connection with these accounts, and some of those named in them have not responded. What is not in dispute is that the people in the film carry real wounds from their years inside Christian music, and that the church should hear them out with honesty rather than defensiveness.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

The church is called to speak the truth in love (Ephesians 4:15) — neither hiding the truth to protect an image, nor using the truth as a weapon against people. When someone is hurt inside the house of God, the answer is not silence or spin, but honesty, repentance, and the grace that restores: “Brethren, if a man be overtaken in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such an one in the spirit of meekness” (Galatians 6:1). Truth and grace must walk together, or neither is truly present.
