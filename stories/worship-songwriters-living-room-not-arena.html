Worship Songwriters Call Churches Back to the Living Room, Not the Arena

By GLM Omnimedia News November 20, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

ATLANTA — As the Passion conference prepares to mark its 30th year this January, two of the most trusted names in modern worship music are asking the church a simple question: can the congregation still hear itself sing?

Brett Younker, a longtime worship leader at Passion City Church in Atlanta and a member of the Passion Music collective, and Paul Baloche, the songwriter behind “Open the Eyes of My Heart” and “Above All,” say many of today’s most popular worship songs are built for the arena, not the local church. When Younker set out to record a solo album in 2023, he wanted something quieter — songs that did not sound as though they were recorded in, or for, a stadium.

On many live worship albums today, Younker told Christianity Today, the sound is pushed so hard there is “no room for the singing.” His album, Keep the Faith, offers what he calls the “songwriter’s versions” of familiar songs such as “No Body” and “Fall Like Rain” — studio recordings that put the melody and the words first, and show that the songs do not need a crowd of thousands, a lighting rig, and a ten-piece band.

Passion helped bring the music of Chris Tomlin, Matt Redman, David Crowder, and Charlie Hall to churches across the country, and it stands today as one of the four main sources of the worship music most used in North American churches, alongside Elevation, Bethel, and Hillsong. The first Passion conference, held in Fort Worth, Texas, in 1997, drew about 2,000 students. By 2000 the gathering had grown past 40,000, and in January 2026 more than 50,000 students filled Globe Life Field in Arlington, Texas.

That scale has changed what many Christians expect a worship song to sound like, and it has changed the work of the worship leader. Even artists who lead worship in arenas admit that a song can stir a crowd of thousands and still fall flat in a church of 200. Baloche said it is fine to be inspired by the big gatherings — he pointed to Tomlin’s 2023 song “Holy Forever” as one that works in both small congregations and megachurches — but he worries that the link between high-production performance and congregational worship has grown too strong, and that it is changing whether church communities sing together at all.

Baloche said worship leaders must create moments that break down the wall between the people on the stage and the people in the pews. He jokingly calls worship sets built on layers of synthesizers and prerecorded backing tracks “church karaoke,” and he said many Christians are ready to step away from the model the arena created. Plenty of people, he said, would be glad to walk into a church and see just a guitar, a piano, and a few vocalists — because worship is not a talent show, but a living room where the family of God sings together.

Neither man is calling for a return to hymns only, or for churches to abandon modern songs. Both still lead worship in megachurches and at conferences, and both believe music written for large settings can serve the local church. Younker said he understands the pull toward hymns, but he values the plain, prayerful language of contemporary songs — he wants some songs that feel devotional rather than academic. His song “The Lord Will Provide,” he noted, worked before 50,000 people in a stadium — and it works with just a guitar.

Part of the problem is practical. Many hit worship songs are written for powerful tenor voices and keyed high, which makes them hard for ordinary church members to sing. Younker recorded some tracks on his album in lower keys, but he said key and instrumentation can only do so much: a singing congregation comes down to leadership.

Britton Wesson, who began leading worship at 15 in a Southern Baptist church in Arkansas and became a full-time worship pastor in 2007, said his generation learned the wrong lessons from the conference era. As leaders copied the arena model, they planned Sunday music as one seamless set and stopped teaching, praying, and reading Scripture between songs so the flow would not break. His generation, he said, learned to let the music do all the talking. The result was polished, concert-like worship — sometimes at the cost of a pastoral voice.

Baloche believes many leaders avoid speaking between songs because it feels risky without practice, and it is easier to play an uninterrupted set. But silence between leader and congregation, he said, only deepens the divide and adds artifice where there should be relationship. Leaders must care about the people first, he said, and cultivate a pastoral posture. His practical advice is simple: try a minimally amplified service, and have the courage to go unplugged one Sunday a month. It may feel uncomfortable at first for churches used to loud speakers and low lights, he said, but it is beautiful to hear the people sing — singing out their prayers together, which is what worship should feel like.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

Worship was never meant to be a performance the people watch; it is a prayer the people sing together. “Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly in all wisdom; teaching and admonishing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing with grace in your hearts to the Lord” (Colossians 3:16). When the music gets so loud the congregation goes silent, something precious has been lost — and it is worth the courage to turn it down and let the people be heard.
