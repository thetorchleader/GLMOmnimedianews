The Evangelical Press Association — a 300-member group of evangelical journalists whose ranks include Christianity Today, Decision magazine, and the Christian Chronicle — has voted on whether to drop the word “Evangelical” from its name and rebrand as the “Christian Communicators Network.”

The group’s board recommended the change back in early August, and voting closed on October 7. In a letter to members, the board said the word “Evangelical” “increasingly carries political, cultural, and sociological meanings that often obscure its theological intent,” while the word “Christian” is “immediately understood around the world.”

Mark Dreistadt, an adviser to the EPA board and president of the Christian marketing firm Infinity Concepts, was blunt about the reason: “evangelical has a lot of baggage.” He said the association knows many people who simply will not use the term anymore, and he admitted that shrinking membership was part of the motivation — a new name, he hopes, might draw new members in.

But the theology isn’t changing. Even under a new name, members would still be required to affirm evangelical beliefs. The name would change; the doctrine would not.

The numbers tell the story of why the board is nervous. A 2024 study commissioned by the EPA itself found that among Protestants whose beliefs about Jesus, the Bible, and evangelism line up with evangelical doctrine, only 61 percent say the term “evangelical Christian” describes them — 39 percent say it does not. And among the American public at large, just 24 percent hold a positive view of the word; 49 percent are neutral and 27 percent view it negatively.

Not everyone is on board with the rebrand. Mark Kellner, a longtime EPA member and journalist, called the move short-sighted and tied to the current political moment — particularly the movement’s association with President Trump. “There is no reason for anyone to be ashamed of the name evangelical,” Kellner said, noting that Trump leaves office in two years, but evangelical churches will outlast him.

Bonnie Kristian, deputy editor of Christianity Today and author of “In Defense of Evangelicalism,” plans to vote no. She argues the new name creates more problems than it solves, since the group would still be evangelical in every meaningful sense — a generic “Christian” label, she says, would mislead mainline, Catholic, and Orthodox believers. “Evangelical,” she maintains, remains “the most useful one we have” to describe theologically conservative Protestants.

The debate reflects a deeper reality documented by Pew Research: the word “evangelical” now functions as both a religious identity and a political label. White evangelicals and Black Protestants share similar theological beliefs but vote very differently — a tension that has loaded the term with political meaning far beyond the church doors.
