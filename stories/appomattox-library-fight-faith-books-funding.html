Christians on Both Sides as a Virginia Library Fight Tests Faith, Books, and Funding

By GLM Omnimedia News November 24, 2026 Source: Religion News Service

APPOMATTOX, Virginia — The sign at the edge of town announces that Appomattox County, home to about 17,000 people, is the place “where our nation reunited.” On an October afternoon, that same small community found itself divided again — this time over religion, LGBTQ+ books, and the future of its public library, according to a report by Religion News Service.

At the October 6 meeting of the J. Robert Jamerson Memorial Library board, the board voted 3-1 to adopt new bylaws that allow it to refuse to comply with state funding requirements, a change that could put part of the library’s money at risk. The state provided more than 30 percent of the library’s budget last year and is projected to cover about 20 percent this year. The meeting had originally been scheduled to proceed without public comment, but vocal opposition from residents changed that.

The new board chair is the Rev. Paul Michael Raymond, pastor of Reformed Bible Church. According to RNS, members of Raymond’s congregation stood guard by the door of the small meeting room — one of them with two handguns visible on his hips — while a retired library employee called Raymond a “wolf in sheep’s clothing.” Raymond told RNS the bylaw changes are simply meant to give the board “a more active oversight position.” “We’re not looking to ban books. We’re not looking to censor books. We’re not looking to discriminate,” he said.

The dispute began months earlier, in June, when three of the five members of the county board of supervisors cited their Christian faith in complaints about a Pride month display at the library, according to emails reviewed by RNS. One supervisor’s church member wrote that the Bible is God’s perfect Word and “says otherwise” about homosexuality and gender, adding that God’s morality “has not changed, and does not depend on the civil law of a given society.” The board’s chair wrote that promoting “the LBGT life style was not consistent with our Biblical beliefs.” The three supervisors did not respond to RNS’s requests for comment.

In July, Raymond and Amanda Chase Scott, a former state senator, were voted onto the library board. At her first meeting in August, Scott raised the idea of eliminating the library’s funding altogether, saying the county would either have to keep what she called “LGBTQ grooming materials” in the library to receive state funding or “defund and completely eliminate all funding from the library.” Scott later told RNS her comments were “taken out of context,” saying she believes there is a workaround, supports a referendum on state funding, and wants to address books displayed in areas “accessible to very young children” rather than remove books.

What makes Appomattox different from many library fights is that Christians are leading the defense of the library as well. Before the October meeting, a group of Christians from Episcopal, Methodist, Presbyterian, and Baptist churches gathered at St. Anne’s Episcopal Church to organize their opposition to the bylaw changes. “We need to defend the reputation of Jesus Christ,” said the Rev. Ruth Perry, a United Methodist minister. Former board member Evelyn Ford put it plainly: “The library is not a place for your specific beliefs to be elevated over others.” Faith groups have played a similar role elsewhere — 60 Arkansas faith leaders opposed new restrictions on children’s books earlier this year, and pastors spoke against censorship at state legislature hearings.

The pattern is now national. Since 2022, religious groups have driven campaigns targeting library funding and access in places including western Michigan, southern Illinois, Ridgeland, Mississippi, St. Marys, Kansas, Pella, Iowa, and Front Royal, Virginia, often related to LGBTQ+ inclusion. In some places the campaigns have succeeded; in others voters have rejected them. John Chrastka, executive director of EveryLibrary, called Appomattox’s proposed bylaw changes “highly irregular” and said risking a portion of the library’s funding formula is “irresponsible.”

Raymond’s own history adds to the tension. In a 2006 magazine article, he wrote, “We ought to strive for a society system that is theocratic.” In 2022, a militia that met in his church hosted a presentation by Tactical Civics, a group that teaches the United States is a Christian nation. Raymond told RNS he is not interested in “forcing Christ” on anybody and said the board would not jeopardize state funding without an alternate plan to pay for the library’s operations. At the October meeting, the board also struck from its bylaws the line describing the library’s role in “the operation of a free and democratic society.” “Democracy is mobocracy,” Raymond said.

For the people caught in the middle, the library is personal. Samantha Johnson, an Appomattox mother of three who is lesbian and identifies as pagan, told RNS it meant a great deal to see Christians opposing the anti-LGBTQ+ efforts. Chance McCaig, who uses they and he pronouns, wrote in a letter supporting the library that inclusive spaces can help prevent LGBTQ+ suicides, adding, “I am 32 now and will tell you it is a miracle I made it this far. I failed at every suicide attempt.” Of the library, McCaig said, “I have found somewhere I can take a deep exhale and actually feel safe being myself.”

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

Scripture commands parents to &ldquo;train up a child in the way he should go&rdquo; (Proverbs 22:6) and commands every believer to love his neighbor as himself (Matthew 22:39). In Appomattox, sincere Christians disagree sharply about how those two duties meet in a public library &mdash; but the witness of Christ is never advanced by intimidation, and it is never harmed by grace. Whatever the board decides about books and budgets, the church&rsquo;s calling is the same: speak the truth in love, guard the little ones, and let the world see Jesus in how we treat our neighbors.
