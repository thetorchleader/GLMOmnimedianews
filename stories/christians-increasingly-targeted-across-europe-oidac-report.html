Attacks on Christians Across Europe Jumped 44 Percent, Watchdog Reports

By GLM Omnimedia News November 26, 2026 Source: Fox News

Attacks targeting Christians across Europe rose sharply last year, according to a new annual report from the Vienna-based Observatory on Intolerance and Discrimination Against Christians in Europe (OIDAC). The group documented 2,465 anti-Christian hate crimes and incidents in 2025 — a 44 percent increase — with France, England and Wales, Germany, and Austria recording the highest totals.

The report's chief concern is not only damaged buildings. OIDAC executive director Anja Tang said attacks on people themselves had risen, and that clergy and those responsible for places of worship were the most attacked, with the numbers increasing most strongly there. The victims, she said, were people who were present, visible, and recognizable as Christians and associated with a church. OIDAC described its own figures as "quite conservative," noting that aggression against individuals is highly underreported across Europe. The group argues that attacks on people deserve separate attention from vandalism, theft, and arson aimed at religious property.

France recorded the sharpest rise. French authorities reported a 70 percent increase in attacks on individuals, the highest in Europe. Tang noted that the French government has not yet published a detailed explanation for the increase.

The Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe's Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights (ODIHR) has increased its focus on anti-Christian hate crimes and published a dedicated guide on the issue in 2025. The office said the guide responded to continuing reports of assaults, threats, vandalism, arson, and desecration aimed at Christian communities, and warned that such incidents can undermine Christians' ability to practice their faith freely. ODIHR told Fox News Digital that underreporting remains "a significant challenge" and that attacks motivated by anti-Christian prejudice should be investigated with the same diligence as attacks motivated by antisemitism, anti-Muslim hatred, or other forms of bias. Political considerations, public perceptions, or prevailing narratives, the office said, should never determine whether an incident is taken seriously — the guiding principle must be equality before the law and equal protection for everyone.

Alessandro Calcagno, deputy general secretary of the Commission of the Bishops' Conferences of the EU (COMECE) and advisor on fundamental rights and freedom of religion within the EU, said OIDAC's work shows the phenomenon exists in Europe and cannot be ignored at the institutional and policy level. He said protecting places of worship cannot be reduced to acts of terrorism, but should also cover vandalism, violence, profanation, disruptions of religious services, arson, and harassment. He backed OIDAC's call for the European Union to appoint a coordinator dedicated to combating anti-Christian hatred, a position COMECE has repeatedly requested. Such a role, he said, would complement existing EU coordinators addressing antisemitism and anti-Muslim hatred, positions he said rightly exist at the EU level.

Lorenzo Vidino, director of the Program on Extremism at George Washington University, said the incidents appear to come from a mix of sources, including anti-religious activists, some individuals from Muslim backgrounds, and ordinary vandalism. He said anti-Christian attacks often receive less public attention than similar incidents involving other faith groups, and that while acts of violence against Muslims and Jews are rightly condemned and prosecuted when they happen, the same is not always true when the target is a church or a Christian site. The law may be neutral, he added, but common perception is not — so those incidents are not prioritized.

Tang said OIDAC is also concerned about migrant communities, including Christians who fled persecution in the Middle East and asylum seekers who left Islam behind and now face severe aggression because of their choice of religion. She said these groups need protection, and that most incidents involving them go unreported out of fear of stigmatization.

Tang also said she was surprised by the number of attacks connected to pro-life convictions and carried out by left-wing extremist groups in German-speaking Europe, which OIDAC categorized as anti-Christian incidents. The level of violence, she said, was significantly greater than she had seen in her time observing these trends. Among the examples she cited: a cafe run by a church in Leipzig, Germany, was forced to close after being vandalized more than 26 times because of its Christian worldview, and an Austrian church was forced to evacuate in the middle of Holy Mass because of a fake bomb placed there by left-wing groups during a prayer connected to a pro-life event.

OIDAC is urging European governments to adopt the recommendations in the OSCE's practical guide on anti-Christian hate crimes, including improving the recording of incidents, strengthening prevention efforts, and expanding support for victims. The group also renewed its call for the EU to appoint a coordinator dedicated to combating anti-Christian hatred.
