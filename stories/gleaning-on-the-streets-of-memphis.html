Memphis Ministry Gives Recovering Addicts Work, Dignity, and a Door Back to Faith

By GLM Omnimedia News October 15, 2026 Source: Christianity Today

In Memphis, Tennessee — a city with some of the nation's highest poverty and crime — a Christian ministry is giving men and women a second chance the old-fashioned way: through hard work. The Business Hub of the Binghampton Development Corporation, a nonprofit founded by Christ Methodist Church of Memphis, hires people coming out of addiction, prison, and homelessness and puts them to work recycling the city's discards — mattresses, Styrofoam, streetlight fixtures, and large-item returns — according to a report by Christianity Today.

The stories behind the warehouse floor are heavy ones. Mariah lost her mother to a police chase at age 11, became her dying father's sole caregiver at 19, and slid into heroin, fentanyl, and methamphetamine addiction that left her homeless. Shemika survived years of childhood abuse, spent 26 years addicted to crack cocaine, and turned to sex work. Justin Frakes lost his wife and young daughter in a car accident, gave up on life, and became homeless. Gary Henderson discovered his brother's body after a suicide, then battled PTSD, depression, and alcoholism after a stroke.

Each of them found the Hub at the bottom of their story, and each has rebuilt since. Mariah now disassembles light fixtures with practiced speed. Shemika, four years sober, has reconnected with her mother and children — her grandchildren call her "the best grandma ever" — and brings addicted women to a local drop-in center. Frakes settled into an apartment within two weeks of starting at the Hub and now manages the warehouse. Henderson, one of the Hub's longest employees, is honing leadership skills as a manager.

The Hub's model is drawn straight from Scripture. Director Andy Kizzee built the program on the Old Testament gleaning laws of Leviticus 19:9–10, which commanded farmers to leave the edges of their fields unharvested so the poor could gather their own livelihoods. The principle: dignified work, not mere handouts. Employees complete a paid, full-time program of three to fifteen months aimed at a living-wage career, with on-the-job training and accountability — including a workplace attendance system and requirements that applicants show up on time for an interview and pass a hard-drug screening.

Ministry runs alongside the machinery. The Hub employs a pastor and psychologist who meets with every worker at least monthly, and case manager Catherine Akridge ferries employees in her own van to record-expungement and driver's license appointments and finds emergency housing for workers facing eviction. Her desk bears a sticker reading, "Jesus loves felons, drunks, prisoners, and addicts." Workers gather Monday mornings to share prayer requests and praises. A third of Hub employees fail their initial drug test and 57 percent arrive with a criminal record — and the ministry takes them anyway.

The results are hard-won and honest, not sugarcoated. According to the report, five-year data shows about 45 percent of Hub employees reach the program's goal of moving from societal exclusion to personal autonomy. Some graduates relapse; some return to prison. Mariah herself lapsed after a breakup and lost her position until she completed rehab again. "They understood," she said, "and they gave me another chance." More than 200 Binghampton residents have been employed so far, and the Hub diverted 2,500 tons of material from landfills in 2026 alone, the report states.

In February 1968, Martin Luther King Jr. came to Memphis to stand with striking sanitation workers, declaring that all labor has dignity — that the person who picks up our garbage is as significant as the physician. The Hub is still proving him right, one reclaimed mattress and one restored life at a time.

Biblical Worldview Takeaway

The Hub in Memphis is living out what the Bible has always taught: work itself is a means of grace. When the law of Moses commanded farmers to leave gleanings for the poor, God dignified labor over dependence — and when Isaiah promised, "Your people will rebuild the ancient ruins... you will be called Repairer of Broken Walls" (Isaiah 58:12), he tied neighborhood renewal to the people of God. The church does not just feed the poor; it restores them to purpose. "For even when we were with you, this we commanded you, that if any would not work, neither should he eat" (2 Thessalonians 3:10) — and likewise, when the lost and broken will work, the church must open the door. As Jesus said, "Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me" (Matthew 25:40).
